The Stolen Archive↗

The Stolen Archive is a virtual exhibition of thirty objects taken during colonial wars, conquests and excavations, from the Benin Bronzes to the Parthenon Marbles. It works as a companion to a museum visit and as a museum of its own, and asks who was able to take these objects, who keeps them and who has managed to get them back.

Contested history made readable: every claim shown with its sources, at the depth the visitor chooses.

Brief
Museums rarely show how colonial-era objects left home, who claims them and who holds them today.
Role
Information modeling and web development; the 2026 redesign on my own.
Method
A metadata model for 30 objects; 810 texts across 3 tours, 3 lengths and 3 reading levels; maps, floor plans and 3D views.
Outcome
A virtual exhibition of 30 objects in 13 rooms on two floors, each with the claims, holdings and sources behind it.
Stack
HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Leaflet, three.js, RDF

Key decisions

  1. 01

    Give every object three positions, how it left, who claims it and who holds it, each with its sources.

    Why
    The objects are contested: the exhibition asks who was able to take them, who keeps them and who has managed to get them back.
    Result
    Visitors read each claim beside its evidence, not a single museum’s label.
  2. 02

    Write nine versions of every object text: three lengths and three levels of complexity, for each of the three tours.

    Why
    Visitors come with different time and needs: a teacher preparing a class visit, someone with thirty minutes, a doctoral student doing research.
    Result
    Each visitor sets length and level once before the tour and can change them on any object page.
  3. 03

    Show how each of the three tours tells the same object, side by side.

    Why
    The tours frame an object differently, as art and history, as conquest, as geography; seeing them together is part of the argument.

The Stolen Archive began as a group project with Corrado Consiglio and Marco Lamorte for Information Modeling and Web Technologies. I reworked it independently in 2026; the version shown here is that rework.

Notes

Visitors pick one of three tours (Timeline, Colonial Conquests, Geo) and choose how long and how complex the texts should be: every object has nine versions of its text for each tour, 810 in all. Each object page explains how the object left, who claims it and who holds it, with the sources for each position. An atlas traces the thirty routes on a map, and a floor plan places the objects in thirteen rooms over two floors.

Developed for Information Modeling and Web Technologies (prof. Fabio Vitali) in the Digital Humanities and Digital Knowledge master's at the University of Bologna. Built in HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with inline SVG maps, 3D views of the objects, an RDF graph for every record, and dark and light themes.
