Unobravo Redesign

Unobravo connects people with therapists over video call. Its website tried to be a marketing page, a guide and an onboarding flow at once, and the app offered no explanation of the service at all. I redesigned both, from interviews to a tested Figma prototype. A concept redesign made for an HCI course; not affiliated with Unobravo.

Trust through transparency: research, three iterations and a measured gain in usability.

Brief
People hesitate to start online therapy: how the service works, matching, prices and privacy were hard to understand.
Role
Solo: research, UX and UI design, prototyping and testing.
Method
Interviews, expert review and think-aloud tests; three design iterations; a summative usability test.
Outcome
SUS rose from 75 to 91.25, with 100% task success and zero errors (4 participants).
Stack
Figma, prototyping, SUS

Key decisions

  1. 01

    Separate explaining the service from starting the test, instead of tidying the existing “How it works” page.

    Why
    No tested user could explain how Unobravo works, and the calls to action dropped people into the questionnaire when they expected an explanation.
    Result
    One structural change fixed three critical issues at once; in the final test every participant understood the service in about a minute and a half.
  2. 02

    Always offer three therapists, each with a “why we chose this therapist for you” explanation.

    Why
    Matching was opaque: the promised three profiles never arrived, and users had to retake the test.
    Result
    Participants singled out the explanation as what made the matching feel trustworthy.
  3. 03

    Let people change their schedule or preferences without retaking the questionnaire.

    Why
    Users repeated the whole test just to change their availability.
    Result
    Changing therapist for scheduling took about 25 seconds, with no errors.
  4. 04

    Replace the app’s sidebar with a tab bar and a dashboard.

    Why
    Navigation was hidden in the sidebar, and there was nowhere to see the next appointment or the therapy so far.
    Result
    Comparing and changing therapist, a task every user failed on the original app, took about 30 seconds.

Unobravo is a leading European online psychology service, founded in Italy in 2019, that matches people with licensed therapists over video call. Many potential users hesitate: they worry about privacy, doubt that online therapy can feel human, and don't understand how the matching works. The goal was to reduce that reluctance and make the service easy to understand and trust, across ages and levels of digital literacy.

Solo project: research, UX and UI design, prototyping, testing
Context

Transparency

Explain data practices, pricing and the matching process clearly, so privacy concerns can be resolved rather than hidden in a footer.

Personalization

Adapt the journey to different needs and levels of readiness, from someone just exploring to someone ready to book.

Motion makes the app feel alive without raising the pulse. The figures in every illustration sway on a slow loop, every screen rises in with a short stagger, and moments that ask something of the user are acknowledged: booking the first session ends with a check that springs in, and each finished questionnaire section blooms one more petal until the Unobravo mark is whole. In the prototype, screens morph into each other with gentle springs, the tab bar pill slides between tabs and answers pop when chosen.

Design system

Show the design systemHide the design system

Behind the screens is a small design system: 46 variables, 14 text styles, 34 components, 47 icons and ten illustrations. Fifteen primitives feed seventeen semantic tokens, and every fill has exactly one job: coral is the only action colour, a coral tint marks selection, clay holds information, sage means something went well, and crimson is kept for errors. Icons are drawn in warm clay and ink, like the text around them, and only turn coral when active. Screens sit on flat paper, with no gradient.

Illustrations

Every illustration is made from those parts only: soft rounded bodies in apricot and sand, heads, and the gentle overprint where they meet. Coral is kept for small accents. Interviews

Show the interviewsHide the interviews

Eight one-on-one interviews of about 30 minutes, held in Italian and recorded with consent, one or more in each segment. They covered how people look for a therapist, what makes a platform feel trustworthy and what stops them from starting.

Preparation

Young adults · 25-34

4
  • 27, musician in Bologna. Several past therapies; time is the barrier now
  • Artist in Amsterdam. Two years of therapy; money and energy keep her from restarting
  • 28, manual worker in Friuli. In therapy for two years after panic attacks
  • Remote secretary near Udine. Has thought about therapy but never started
  • →People ask for help late, so the search happens under pressure
  • →Too much choice blocks first-timers and anyone in distress
  • →An introductory session is essential to check fit
  • →Calm and warm, never clinical
Contemplation

Mid-career adults · 35-44

2
  • 39, school assistant in Friuli. Past psychotherapy; copes alone until breaking point
  • 37, art teacher in Friuli. Long history of therapy, including online during COVID
  • →Therapy is valued, yet postponed until distress is unmanageable
  • →Trust comes from visible CVs, a clear approach and reviews
  • →A questionnaire should help reflection, not feel like a test
  • →Prices must be clear and sustainable over time
Precontemplation

Mature adults · 45-55

2
  • Office worker in Friuli. Two therapists: only the second was the right fit
  • Factory worker in Friuli. Considered therapy once, never started
  • →Finding the right person takes trial and error
  • →Seriousness and professionalism matter more than friendliness
  • →Family and friends are the first gateway to help
  • →Extra tools only if the therapist suggests them
Across all eight interviews

What we heard, and what it changed

  1. 01Help comes late, and in a hurryAlmost everyone waits until things feel overwhelming, so the first visit happens under urgency and fatigue.Design → A crisis banner on every questionnaire screen and a short guided path
  2. 02Too much choiceLong therapist lists are impossible to judge without experience, and harder still when distressed.Design → Three curated proposals with “why we suggest them”; browsing stays available
  3. 03Fit over credentialsFeeling listened to matters more than age, gender or method, but people want to know the approach before they start.Design → Approach, experience and first slot on every card, plus a free introductory call
  4. 04Transparency builds trustCVs, clear prices, reviews and an honest explanation of how matching works.Design → Pricing on the home screen and a “why this match” note on each proposal
  5. 05Nothing should feel finalChanging therapist or redoing the questionnaire without penalty lowers the fear of committing.Design → Change therapist without retaking the whole test
  6. 06Calm, not clinicalSoft colours and rounded shapes; neither hospital-like nor a playful wellness app.Design → A restrained palette, one accent and gentle motion

Cast and personas

Show the personasHide the personas

One protagonist and seven supporting personas, built from the interviews and spread across the three age segments and stages of change. Each is rated 1-5 on six traits, so the cast shows where the design has to stretch.

Protagonist · “A new start”

Luca Taglioni

29, Graphic designer, Milan, Young adult · Preparation
“There is hope, even when your brain tells you there isn't.”

Anxiety, panic attacks and poor sleep, mostly from work pressure. Friends in therapy encouraged him and he feels ready. He expects polished, personal apps and dislikes rigid, bureaucratic flows. He will invest time and money if the platform feels trustworthy and human.

Use case. On a Sunday morning Luca opens the app a friend recommended, filters for therapists who work on anxiety and stress, compares availability, takes the questionnaire and books a free introductory session for later that week.

  • →A clean, professional interface that still feels warm, not clinical
  • →Filters and recommendations tailored to anxiety and sleep
  • →Booking with availability, cost and policies up front
  • →Reassuring microcopy for a first-time therapy user
Close to the target, with a sharper need

R01 · addresses I01, I02

Separate explaining from converting

A non-transactional “Scopri Unobravo” path explains the service, matching, costs and privacy before anyone is asked to start the test.

R02 · addresses I02

Calmer calls to action

“Scopri come funziona” sits next to “Inizia”, so users choose between exploring and committing.

R03 · addresses I03

Make matching explicit

Always three proposals, each with a “Why we chose this therapist for you” explanation.

R04 · addresses I04, I05

Decouple preferences from matching

Change schedule or preferences without retaking the full questionnaire.

The biggest change was structural, not visual: separating “understand the service” from “start the test” fixed three critical issues at once. For a mental-health service, trust comes from transparency: showing how matching works, what it costs and what happens to your data.

The final version adds what a real product would need next: crisis support and health-data consent in the questionnaire, empty and error states, and the online session itself. The samples were small (three to four people per round), so the next step would be a larger test across all age segments.
