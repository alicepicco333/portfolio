The Meme Ontology↗

MEMO is an ontology of internet memes, from my master's research Towards an Ontology of Memes (supervisor Fabio Vitali), which I began as a visiting research fellow at the Institute of Network Cultures in Amsterdam, for its Viral Image Cultures project.

A messy cultural archive made queryable, with the limits of its automatic classification stated.

Brief
A large, messy cultural dataset with no structured model to classify, link or query it.
Role
Sole researcher: data collection, ontology, classification and visualization.
Method
About 5,000 Know Your Meme entries scraped; an OWL ontology informed by FRBR; CLIP for semi-automatic classification.
Outcome
A published ontology (206,923 triples) with documentation, eleven interactive visualizations, a SPARQL explorer linked to Wikidata and the full thesis as an indexed reader.
Stack
Python, CLIP, OWL 2 / Protégé, SPARQL (Comunica), D3.js

Key decisions

  1. 01

    Separate the meme template from its variants, following FRBR.

    Why
    Treating every image as the same kind of thing flattens how variants transform their template.
    Result
    It made the decoupling of views and variants visible, and led to a transformation typology that neither FRBR nor iconography provides.
  2. 02

    Label all 5,000 images with CLIP, and annotate only 50 variants by hand.

    Why
    Hand annotation cannot scale to the corpus; zero-shot labels can.
    Result
    The automatic layer proved the weakest: next time I would keep only the labels several prompts agree on, and flag the rest for review.

Internet memes are one of the largest and least formally organized bodies of visual culture of the twenty-first century. Know Your Meme documents them in depth, but as a community wiki: there was no formal, theory-grounded model to classify meme templates, their visual properties and the way their variants transform them. MEMO (the MEMe Ontology) is that model, built and tested on a corpus of 5,000 entries.

A formal model

Extend bibliographic (FRBR/LRM) and iconological (Panofsky) frameworks so memes can be classified without flattening their cultural complexity.

Culture at corpus scale

Make structural patterns in meme culture (formats, platforms, periods, variants) visible and queryable, following Manovich’s cultural analytics.

Four stages, each producing the structured input for the next, adapted from Manovich’s operations for cultural analytics.

Stage 1 · data

Three datasets

A random sample of 5,000 Know Your Meme entries with images and metadata; D0, a purposive subset of 50; and 391 variant images from the galleries of 43 D0 entries.

Stage 2 · ontology

Two axes of classification

An OWL 2 DL ontology built in Protégé along FRBR/LRM levels (MemeIdea, MemeConcept, VariantInstance) and Panofsky’s levels of meaning, reusing Wikidata, schema.org and Dublin Core.

Stage 3 · features

CLIP and manual annotation

CLIP ViT-B/32 zero-shot labels for image type, subject, text and colour on all 5,000 images; hand annotation of cultural references and of how 50 variants transform their template.

Stage 4 · visualization

A public web platform

D3.js visualizations of the whole corpus, D0 and the variants, alongside the ontology documentation, an in-browser SPARQL endpoint and the thesis text.

  1. RQ1
    FRBR, extended, fits memesIdea, template and variant are distinct entities that carry different information, and the transformation typology adds what neither FRBR nor iconography models.
  2. RQ2
    CLIP helps, but cannot be trusted aloneImage-type labels are plausible (Cartoon 45.3%, Photograph 44.8%), but zero-shot classification always picks a label; 34.4% of subjects fell back to Unknown and the rest lack ground truth.
  3. RQ3
    Patterns only visible at scaleDurable foundational formats, the move from static images to short-form video, and format as a predictor of reach.
  4. RQ4
    Folksonomy as evidenceUsing Wikidata’s publisher property for platform keeps community attributions as assertions rather than claims of objective provenance.

The ontology works best where it is most theoretical: separating the template from its variants is what made the decoupling of views and variants visible at all.

The weakest layer is the automatic one. Next time I would run several classification passes with different prompts and keep only the labels they agree on, flagging the rest for review: fewer assertions, but ones the analysis can rely on.

Publishing the thesis, the data and the queries together turned a written argument into something people can check and explore themselves.
